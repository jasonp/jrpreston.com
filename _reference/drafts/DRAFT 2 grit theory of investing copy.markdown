# The real Moneyball for startups
# Grit theory of venture funding

Most VCs agree that when they fund an early-stage startup they are funding the team more than the product. That's because most VCs know that the initial product is almost never the right one, and that it will have to evolve until it finds product/market fit and can grow from there. 

Which means that the business of funding startups is a business that involves a lot of psychology. Yet many, if not all, VC funds appear to filter their investments by domain instead of by personality -- the idea is usually that some changes are creating new opportunities in a particulary industry. Perhaps the most famous example is Andreesen Horowitz' "software is eating the world," or more directly their recently announced Blockchain fund designed to invest in blockchain startups. 

So while many VCs do make bets on the team more than the idea, they don't have a good set of measurable ways to evaluate the team. Simply using "gut feel" is problematic because it increases the odds of unsconcious bias and heuristics driving their decisions, but many VCs invest by gut nonetheless. It is after all more useful than trying to pick by market opportunity. As a result the good VCs have to evaluate the team by proxy: through their idea, through their business plan, through their references. 

But what if they could measure the team more directly? Would that open the door to a better performing strategy for making startup investments? 

Many people have played with this idea before. In 2011, Dan Frommer talked with several prominent VCs like [Fred Wilson](http://avc.com), [Paul Graham](http://www.paulgraham.com/), and [Ben Horowitz](https://a16z.com/author/ben-horowitz/) about using data to make startup investments and reported the results in a post called [Moneyball for tech startups](http://www.fromedome.com/2011/09/moneyball-for-tech-startups/). Each answer seems to say that nobody has found a useful way to collapse all the data around investments -- valuation, stage, market size, run rate, timing, and so on -- into any useful comparable format for making decisions. 

What strikes me is that many of the attempts to "moneyball" startup investments between then and now have tried to turn things about the market or the opportunity into data that can be crunched, when what you really care about is the team. And not just the team, but *what are the psychological characteristics of the individuals* who make up the team. 

There are a few companies such as [YouNoodle](https://ww2.younoodle.com/startup-programs/), which began by offering evaluations of investment opportunities to VCs but has since evolved to offer consulting to companies that want to run various kinds of startup competitions. This suggests that their methodology wasn't very successful at picking winners.

Other VC funds have spent time experimenting with adding a kind of psychological screen to their process. This is a step in the right direction, but VCs who are doing this are understandably cagey about what is involved. For one, if they are successful they'd like to keep a competitive advantage against other firms, and for another, the less candidates know about psychological screening, the more effective it will be. But there is a lot to psychology and not a lot is likely to be predictive in investing. 

Over the past six years I've had the opportunity to build a unique perspective on this. I co-founded a startup called [Dent](http://dentthefuture.com) (Steve Jobs: "put a dent in the universe"), which is a conference dedicated to understanding what enables some people to do world-changing things, and then pushing today's emerging and established leaders to think and act bigger. 

I've read dozens of books and reserach papers on leadership, psychology, motivation, and charisma. Every year I talk to hundreds of fascinating people about what they're doing, how they're doing it, why they're successful at it (or why they failed), and make judgments about which stories and lessons to bring to our stage. 

So I have a strong set of ideas about what makes a person capable of putting a dent in the universe. Here is a framework for Moneyballing startup investments that I call the grit theory of venture funding. 

There are two really fascinating psychological theories that are relevant here. The first one is, of course, [about grit](https://smile.amazon.com/Grit-Passion-Perseverance-Angela-Duckworth-ebook/dp/B010MH9V3W/). The psychologist behind this theory is Angela Duckworth from the University of Pennsylvania. 

The idea at its core is that success requires both persistence (Angela calls this "grit") and talent, but that persistence is twice as important. 

Since grit can be measured in several different ways, we can set up a scale by which we measure people's "grittiness" and draw an association to certain kinds of success. When we do this, we discover that grit is a remarkably powerful predictor of success in a wide array of circumstances. For eaxmple, it excels at answering the famously difficult question: which incoming freshmen are going to drop out of West Point? 

The other psychological theory is from Carol Dweck, and it draws a distinction between two [different mindsets](https://smile.amazon.com/Mindset-Psychology-Carol-S-Dweck-ebook/dp/B000FCKPHG/): a growth mindset, and a fixed mindset. Having a growth mindset is preferred -- a person with a growth mindset appropriately sees their failures as opportunities to learn and improve, instead of permanent indicators of what they are capable of. Again, this effect can be measured in a few relatively straightforward ways. 

These two traits are hugely important in startup founders, because building a successful startup requires failing a lot and seeing each failure as an opportunity to improve. 

But there is a third thing that all successful startups do that stems from something startup founders have: they solve real problems. The funny thing about startup problems, as YCombinator's co-founder Paul Graham has pointed out many times, is that they are counter-intuitive. Setting out to think of startup ideas is a pretty good way to get bad startup ideas. Worse, you will get bad startup ideas that seem like they are probably good ones, which means you will waste time and money on them if given the chance. 

Instead, the right thing to do is to notice a startup idea. The ability to notice things is not usually constrained to startup ideas. Great stand up comedians are master noticers of things. Probably many of you have friends who notice things all the time that suprise you, even though you could have noticed it anytime. 

There is a big difference between seeing something, knowing something, and noticing something. And only noticing really matters for startups.   

Each of these three traits are things that can be learned. Most things that appear to be natural talent are in fact the result of hidden work and feedback loops, which is one of the more significant things I've learned through six years of research and conversations with some of the most amazingly ambitious and successful people. There is some significant scientific evidence to back up this point of view, including the tendency for people to [view their managers differently](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fPpr6jXdE-o) based on whether or not the managers appear to be working hard. 

But I am not convinced that these traits can be effectively learned at the time scale that you need to learn things in startups. That's because psychological changes are difficult to motivate in people, and ultimately nobody can make those changes but the person in question. Also there is no telling in advance what the right amount of pressure will be to cause change. Too much and they will shut down. Too little and they will blindly puruse an incorrect path.

So while I think that founders with strong technical expertise in a given field can learn to be good noticers, have growth mindsets, and increase their grit, it's a much better bet in startup investing to look for the psychological traits and plan on founders gaining (or hiring for) technical expertise if they lack it. 

Finally and perhaps obviously, a person has to be *interested* in founding a venture-scale startup, which is actually very uncommon. There are plenty of people who share these three traits who aren't interested in building the next Facebook because that comes at great personal cost. Luckily this filter is built-in, because the people looking for VC funding are by definition the people interested in following that path. 

When you have a venture-scale founder or set of founders who notice things, have a large amount of grit, and a growth mindset, I think that is a very good bet for a VC regardless of industry. It would probably be a good idea to make early stage startup investments on these particular criteria because they can be measured and they appear to be predictive.

This is an opportunity for some VCs to take a more defined and potentially less biased approach to a part of startup investment that has traditionally been very vague.

Scratchwork:

A notable exception might be YCombinator, which as a model makes lots of small bets on founding teams and tries their best to help them succeed. But even YCombinator really only funds technology startups, because there are certain kinds of industries where making lots of small captial outlays is not a good investment strategy. So their theory is still an industry theory: in software and some hardware innovation, the opportunity is in making lots of small bets and trying to goose the odds of their success. 

Being interested in founding something really big is an important filter, because if you simply looked to fund people who meet the three psychological qualifications, you would end up finding a lot of people who really want to be running side projects or traditional small businesses. Things at the small end of the entrepreneurship scale also benefit from growth mindsets and grit, but they're still not very interesting to a venture fund, because they're not going to generate the kind of returns that a VC needs to see.
