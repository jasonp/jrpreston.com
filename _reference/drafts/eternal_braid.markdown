# The Most Important Lesson in Gödel Escher Bach

 Maybe the two most important?
 1. Complex behavior emerges from simple mechanisms
 2. Isomorphisms: symbols and systems are not the realities they represent
 Which is more important?

March, 2017

Perhaps as long as a year ago, I read a post on Hacker News asking for the books what most influenced people's thinking, and found near the top of the list a book I'd never heard of: [Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid][1]. A month ago I finally picked it up at a bookstore and have been obsessively reading it ever since. 

GEB, as it is commonly abbreviated, is a seven hundred and fifty page tome, filled with illustrations, formal systems, genetics, and computer code. Written in 1979, it holds up extremely well after nearly forty years, and though I can't speak much from personal knowledge about the progress of Artificial Intelligence research since it was written, it seems to me that the basic philosophical assertions GEB contains still hold a lot of truth. 

I believe the most important lesson in the book is that complex behaviors arise from simple mechanisms. This simple (ha!) idea takes a lot of time to take root, and its counterintuitive results affect the way you think about all kinds of things ranging from economics to the UI design in your startup product. 

I am attending a conference this April called [COFES][2], which stands for the Conference On the Future of Engineering Software. The conference mails a book-group book to attendees ahead of the gathering, and this year's selection is a book called [Complexity][3], by M. Mitchell Waldrop. 

The book chronicles the origins and rise of the study of complexity in all branches of modern science, and begins with the story of Stanford's Brian Arthur. Brian wondered if the simple, mathematical, clean rules of Economic _actually described_ the obviously complex and interrelated behaviors of humans, markets, and goods in the real world. It seemed that maybe, in the course of turning Economics from astrology into science, the leading minds of the field may have sucked from economic theory all power to represent and predict the way the world actually worked. 

This is immediately analogous to the central question in GEB: namely, how can complex, strange, contradictory, and beautiful behavior arise from a human brain, if a human brain is in fact just a wet bag of neurons firing mechanically? 

It seems impossible, at least without giving in to complete determinism or allowing some nod to an as-yet-undiscovered aspect of quantum physics. But the answer is in fact much simpler. All such complex systems operate on many different "levels." 

In the case of Economics, there is the macroeconomic flow of resources on the top level, and the microeconomic, individual purchase or barter decisions on the basic level. For brains, there is the highest level in which thinking takes place, with concepts, words, memories, and the like, all the way down to the individual process of electronic impulses building up inside neuron receptors and causing chain reactions of firing neurons following simple, straightforward, and unchanging rules of physics.

Any particular "level" of any such system shares little meaning with any other level of the system. You can describe the behavior on whichever level you like -- but it's as pointless to try and understand what a brain is thinking by following synapse impulses as it is to predict currency behavior by looking at what tourists are exchanging at kiosks in airports. 

This may seem insignificant, but your assumptions about any system that you operate in (office, industry, field of science) will govern your behavior in it. If you think that fundamentally any system is just the sum of its parts, you're going to treat it differently than if you believe there are emergent--and brand new--properties on successive levels of abstraction.
