# Grit theory of venture funding

Most VCs agree that when they fund an early-stage startup they are funding the team more than the product. That's because most VCs know that the initial product is almost never the right one, and that it will have to evolve until it finds product/market fit and can grow from there. 

Which means that the business of funding startups is a business that involves a lot of psychology. Yet many, if not all, VC funds have investment thesises that are built around domain specific theories -- the idea that some identifiable changes are creating opportunities in certain industries. Perhaps the most famous example is Andreesen Horowitz' "software is eating the world."

A notable exception might be YCombinator, which as a model makes lots of small bets on founding teams and tries their best to help them succeed. But even YCombinator really only funds technology startups, because there are certain kinds of industries where making lots of small captial outlays is not a good investment strategy. So their theory is still an industry theory: in software and some hardwarer innovation, the opportunity is in making lots of small bets and trying to goose the odds of their success. 

I think an interesting approach to VC investment might be what I will call the grit theory of venture funding. 

There are two really fascinating psychological theories that are relevant here. The first one is, of course, about "grit." The psychologist behind this theory is Angela Duckworth from the University of Pennsylvania. 

The idea at its core is that success requires both persistence (Angela calls this "grit") and talent, but that persistence is twice as important. 

Since grit can be measured in several different ways, we can set up a scale by which we measure people's "grittiness" and draw an association to certain kinds of success. When we do this, we discover that grit is a remarkably powerful predictor of success in a wide array of circumstances, including the famously difficult to predict question: who is going to drop out of West Point? 

The other psychological theory is from Carol Dweck, and it draws a distinction between two different mindsets: a growth mindset, and a fixed mindset. Having a growth mindset is preferred -- a person with a growth mindset appropriately sees their failures as opportunities to learn and improve, instead of permanent indicators of what they are capable of.

These two traits are hugely important in startup founders, because building a successful startup requires failing a lot and seeing each failure as an opportunity to improve. 

But there is a third thing that all successful startups do that stems from something startup founders have: they solve real problems. The funny thing about startup problems, as YCombinator's co-founder Paul Graham has pointed out many times, is that they are counter-intuitive. Setting out to think of startup ideas is a pretty good way to get bad startup ideas. Worse, you will get bad startup ideas that seem like they are probably good ones, which means you will waste time and money on them if given the chance. 

Instead, the right thing to do is to notice a startup idea. The ability to notice things is not usually constrained to startup ideas. Great stand up comedians are master noticers of things. Probably many of you have friends who notice things all the time that suprise you, even though you could have noticed it anytime. 

There is a big difference between seeing something, knowing something, and noticing something. And only noticing really matters for startups. 

Finally, just because someone notices things doesn't mean that they're going to be a good startup founder. For one thing, a person has to be *interested* in founding a startup, and that's not usually a very sane thing to want to do. 

When you have a founder or set of founders who notice things, have a large amount of grit, and a growth mindset, I think that is a very good bet. It would probably be a good idea to make early stage startup investments on these criteria.
