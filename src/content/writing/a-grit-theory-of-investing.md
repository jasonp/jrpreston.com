The first time I sat in a board meeting that was going badly, I thought I understood what was wrong. I was new at this. I had read the Dent playbook. I had absorbed Stewart's patient questions. I believed, the way you believe early things, that the way you fix a hard conversation is to be smarter about it.

I was wrong. The way you fix a hard board meeting is to have been paying attention for six weeks before the meeting happened. Everything I needed to know about what was about to go wrong was already visible in the weekly updates, in the hiring pace, in the way the founder answered the one question I had asked about runway.

> Grit doesn't mean you don't quit. It means you don't quit for the wrong reasons. The wrong reasons are noisy. The right ones are quiet and come from the numbers.

What I mean by a grit theory of investing is this: the people who do well in venture are the ones who can tell the difference between being stubborn and being wrong. That's it. That's the whole thing. Everything else — the deal flow, the pattern matching, the term sheet craft — is downstream of that one distinction.

## Stubborn or wrong

A thing I've noticed over the last five years is that the founders I want to back almost always have a story about the second time they were wrong. Not the first time. The first time is free. Anyone can be wrong once and recover. The second time is when character shows up — when the cost of admitting the first mistake is already paid, and all that's left is the question of whether you're going to repeat it.
